# DeepSeek-R1

**DeepSeek — R1 and R1-Zero, January 2025.** Open weights, published method,
reported training cost far below Western frontier equivalents. R1-Zero
demonstrated that reasoning behaviour could emerge from reinforcement learning
against verifiable rewards with no supervised reasoning traces.

## Inversion

Three assumptions at once.

**Cost:** that frontier-adjacent capability required nine-figure training budgets.
**Access:** that reasoning models were a proprietary asset of a small number of
labs.
**Method:** that eliciting reasoning required curated human demonstrations —
R1-Zero got it from RL alone.

The constraint that disappeared was the capital moat around the reasoning
capability, roughly four months after it was first demonstrated.

## Incentives

Immediate for every actor outside the frontier labs: enterprises wanting
on-premises deployment, states wanting sovereign capability, researchers wanting
something to study, and competitors wanting a free base to build on.

Directly against the interests of the labs whose valuations assumed a durable
capability lead, and against the assumption underlying export controls — that
restricting hardware restricts capability. If method and efficiency can partly
substitute for compute, the policy instrument is weaker than designed.

## Inflection

Real, with an unusually short and legible market reaction — the sharp Nvidia and
AI-infrastructure selloff of 27 January 2025 was the market repricing the
assumption that capability requires unbounded compute.

Whether that repricing was correct remains open: the counter-argument (cheaper
inference expands total demand rather than shrinking it) is a Jevons-paradox
claim, and which of the two dominates has not yet resolved.

## Lesson for Kyros

The best recent case of an inflection whose consequence is **geopolitical and
financial rather than technical**. The capability was not new; o1 had shown it.
What changed was who possessed it and at what price.

Screening questions this case argues for:
- Does this collapse a cost by an order of magnitude, or merely reduce it?
- Does it move a capability across a *national* or *institutional* boundary?
- Does it invalidate the assumption behind an existing policy instrument?

An affirmative on the third is rare and always significant, because policy moves
slower than technology and the gap becomes a positioning window.
