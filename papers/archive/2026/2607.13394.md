###### Abstract

Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) offer a promising alternative to reward-maximizing reinforcement learning (RL) for learning large reasoning models.
GFlowNets encourage diverse reasoning paths by matching reward distributions rather than collapsing to dominant modes.
Recent work shows promise on math and code benchmarks, but scaling GFlowNet-style RL to modern post-training pipelines remains difficult. In the regimes that matter for LLM post-training, model size, rollout horizon, reward noise, and distributed-systems complexity all increase together, and a learned prompt-conditional partition function can become a source of gradient instability and engineering overhead rather than a useful normalizer.
We address these challenges through systematic analysis to reveal what components are truly necessary for GFlowNet-based RL at scale. Our main finding is that the learned partition function, previously treated as essential, can be replaced by an in-batch Monte Carlo estimate computed from the rollout group already required for training. Based on this observation, we propose GFlowRL, a streamlined GFlowNet-style RL algorithm that removes the auxiliary partition network entirely while preserving the reward-distribution-matching objective. The recipe is completed by two stabilizers: importance-sampling correction for rollout/trainer drift, and asymmetric flow-gap clipping for outlier residuals.
GFlowRL improves both stability and performance compared to prior work. It exceeds all counterparts on math, code, and adversarial red-teaming benchmarks, reaching a Codeforces rating of 2048 at the 14B scale (within Elo of o3-mini) and attaining the highest average ASR@1 on AdvBench and HarmBench, outperforming the previous SOTA multi-turn attacker in a regime where FlowRL, a previous GFlowNet-style RL method, diverges. The same recipe transfers to all evaluated MoE configurations up to 235B parameters, where FlowRL again fails to converge. To our knowledge, GFlowRL is the first GFlowNet-style RL algorithm to scale stably across both dense and sparse architectures for large reasoning models. Code will be released at https://github.com/microsoft/gflowrl.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the defining post-training paradigm for the current generation of large language models. The most capable reasoning systems deployed today, including OpenAI o1 (OpenAI, 2024) and o3 (OpenAI, 2025), DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025a), and Gemini (Kavukcuoglu, 2025), all rely on RL as a central component of their post-training pipelines. The effectiveness of this stage directly determines model performance on high-value tasks such as mathematical reasoning, competitive programming, scientific problem solving, and agentic decision making. As models scale and reasoning chains grow longer, the choice of RL algorithms has become a first-order architectural decision.

The dominant RL approaches, including PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), OMD (Kimi-Team et al., 2025) and their variants, are fundamentally reward maximizing. They concentrate probability mass on high reward modes, often collapsing solution diversity (Xu et al., 2025). In chain-of-thought reasoning, where multiple valid solution paths are essential for generalization and robustness, this behavior introduces a structural limitation. Existing mitigations, such as entropy bonuses (Shen, 2026), adaptive clipping, and token-level exploration, treat diversity (Yu et al., 2025b) as a post hoc regularizer while leaving the core reward maximizing objective unchanged.

Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets) (Bengio et al., 2023; Bengio et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2024) offer a fundamentally different paradigm: instead of maximizing the expected reward, they learn policies that sample trajectories in proportion to their reward. When applied to LLM post-training, this distribution-matching perspective directly addresses mode collapse, encouraging coverage of all high-reward reasoning paths rather than concentrating on one. Recent approaches such as FOR (Yu et al., 2025a) and FlowRL (Zhu et al., 2026) are among the first to introduce this idea into LLM reasoning, adopting a trajectory balance objective with a learned approximation to the partition function . In FlowRL, is parameterized as a three-layer MLP on top of the prompt’s final hidden state, whereas in FOR it is represented as a scalar shared across all prompts.

The scaling problem.
While FlowRL shows the promise of GFlowNet-style training, deploying it in the regimes that matter for modern LLM post-training remains challenging. Even on dense backbones, training must operate under a short optimization horizon, long reasoning rollouts, large pretrained policies, and tight distributed-systems budgets. In this setting, jointly training a new initialized partition model alongside the policy becomes increasingly brittle as scale grows: gradient spikes become more frequent, the auxiliary module adds optimizer and synchronization overhead, and the learned normalizer lags behind the much larger pretrained policy. MoE routing (Guo et al., 2025a; Achiam et al., 2023; Grattafiori et al., 2024; Kimi-Team et al., 2025; Kavukcuoglu, 2025) amplifies these issues further by introducing nondeterminism and implicit off-policy mismatch between sampling and training (Thinking Machines, 2025; Yao et al., 2025), but MoE is best viewed as the hardest stress test of a more general scaling bottleneck rather than the root cause itself.

The root cause. We attribute these failures to a key design choice: the learnable partition function . In LLM post-training, there is a fundamental asymmetry between the two components being optimized. The policy is initialized from a pretrained model with billions of well-trained parameters and requires only modest updates, whereas is randomly initialized and must learn a complex quantity from scratch within the same limited training steps. As a result, behaves largely as a source of noise for much of training, causing the trajectory balance objective to be dominated by variance rather than the reward signal. Section 3.1 provides a detailed analysis of the gradient behavior and training dynamics. Empirically, we have observed two consistent patterns: (1) replacing with a random noise yields comparable performance, suggesting that it contributes little to modeling; and (2) FlowRL exhibits gradient norms orders of magnitude larger than GRPO, leading to significant optimization instability without bringing any visible gains in accuracy.

Based on this analysis, we propose GFlowRL, a streamlined GFlowNet-based RL algorithm that does not use the learned partition function. In its place, we use a simple yet effective in-batch Monte Carlo estimate of , estimated directly from the rollout group already sampled during GRPO for each prompt. This simple change has three consequences: (i) the gradient norms remain the same scale as that of GRPO, restoring training stability; (ii) the auxiliary network and its optimizer state in distributed training are all removed; and (iii) the algorithm uses a nearly identical infrastructure of GRPO, while retaining the reward-distribution-matching semantics of trajectory balance. The recipe is completed by two stabilizers: importance-sampling correction for rollout/trainer drift, and asymmetric flow-gap clipping for outlier residuals. To our knowledge, GFlowRL is the first GFlowNet-based RL algorithm to scale stably across both dense and sparse architectures.

In summary, our contributions are threefold:

1. 

We identify the use of the learned partition function as a key source of instability in GFlowNet-based RL training for LLMs. We provide empirical evidence that offers almost no benefit for post-training and instead can make optimization unnecessarily challenging.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Why the Learned Partition Function Fails

While the TB objective is theoretically principled, we argue that its standard instantiation is poorly suited to the LLM post-training regime. The crux is a learning-horizon mismatch between the two components of the joint optimization.

The learning-horizon mismatch.
The policy is initialized from a pretrained LLM checkpoint with billions of parameters that encode rich linguistic and mathematical priors from trillions of tokens of pretraining (Qwen-Team, 2024). Post-training RL needs only to refine this strong prior, and typically runs for a few hundred gradient updates. The partition function , in contrast, is a freshly initialized network (e.g., 3-layer MLP as in FlowRL) that must learn a complex, prompt-conditional quantity from scratch within the same short window. This asymmetry means that throughout most of training, is effectively a random function of the prompt.

This is a natural fit for LLM post-training, where the rollout group is already the unit of computation.

## 4 Experiment

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Models, datasets, and metrics.
We evaluate GFlowRL across three reasoning domains. For math, we train Qwen2.5-7B/32B (Qwen-Team, 2024) on six standard benchmarks (AIME2024/2025 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2024; Zhang and Math-AI, 2025), AMC2023 (Zhang and Math-AI, 2023), MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), Olympiad (He et al., 2024)), reporting Avg@16 (Yu et al., 2025b; Zhu et al., 2026). For coding, we train Deepseek-R1-Distilled-Qwen-7B/14B (Guo et al., 2025a) on LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2025), Codeforces (Penedo et al., 2025) (Rating/Percentile, following (Luo et al., 2025)), and HumanEval+ (Chen et al., 2021). For LLM safety, we use SEMA (Feng et al., 2026) on AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023) and HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), reporting ASR@1. MoE scaling uses Qwen3-30B-A3B and Qwen3-235B-A22B.

Baselines and implementation.
We compare against FlowRL (Zhu et al., 2026) as the distribution-matching baseline, and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), GRPO/GRPO+ (Shao et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025), and REINFORCE++ (Hu et al., 2025) as reward-maximization baselines. Our PyTorch implementation builds on verl, deepcoder, FlowRL, and SEMA, using their default training configurations unless otherwise noted. Detailed information such as dataset and framework links are provided in Appendix D.

Despite the increase in parameters, training remains stable end-to-end: GFlowRL improves over the backbone by average points (83.35 vs. 81.29) and over GRPO by (vs. 82.40), with gains on every benchmark. To our knowledge, this is the first GFlowNet-style RL method trained stably on a 235B MoE, and the fact that the recipe transfers suggests the streamlined estimator removes a fundamental scaling bottleneck.

## 5 Related Work

RL for LLM reasoning. RLHF established post-training with PPO-style optimization and a reference-policy regularizer (Ouyang et al., 2022; Schulman et al., 2017). Recent reasoning systems shift to verifiable rewards on math and code, enabling critic-free or group-based optimization at scale (GRPO, REINFORCE++, DAPO, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi k1.5, DeepCoder) (Shao et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2025a; Kimi-Team et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). These methods maximize expected reward and tend to concentrate mass on a small set of dominant trajectories.

GFlowNets and distribution matching. GFlowNets sample compositional objects in proportion to reward rather than greedily maximizing it (Bengio et al., 2021; Bengio et al., 2023; Malkin et al., 2022). The closest prior work for LLM post-training is FOR and FlowRL (Yu et al., 2025a; Zhu et al., 2026), both of which instantiate the trajectory balance objective with a learned prompt-conditional partition network. GFlowRL stays in this distribution-matching line but replaces that auxiliary network with a rollout-based estimate, motivated by its instability when jointly trained with a frontier-scale policy.

In-batch estimation and variance reduction. Our estimator connects to a long line of variance-reduced score-function estimators, from REINFORCE baselines (Williams, 1992) to leave-one-out and control-variate methods (Richter et al., 2020; Grathwohl et al., 2018), and in LLM RL to group-relative normalization in GRPO and REINFORCE++ (Shao et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025). We use the rollout group to estimate the partition term implied by the distribution-matching objective and inject it as a stop-gradient baseline, structurally analogous to a GRPO group-mean baseline but applied to the trajectory-balance residual rather than the advantage. Extended discussion in Appendix C.

## 6 Conclusion

Distribution-matching RL is appealing for LLM reasoning because it preserves multiple high-reward solution paths, but our results suggest the standard GFlowNet recipe does not transfer cleanly to modern post-training. The bottleneck is not the trajectory-balance objective itself but the learned partition function: in the short-horizon regime of LLM RL, a randomly initialized must be learned from scratch alongside a large pretrained policy that only needs modest refinement, turning the partition term into a source of noise, gradient instability, and systems overhead. We proposed GFlowRL, which replaces the learned partition network with an in-batch Monte Carlo estimate from the rollout group already required for training, combined with importance sampling and flow-gap clipping for stability. The result is a simpler algorithm with a GRPO-compatible pipeline that retains the reward-distribution-matching perspective: it matches or improves on FlowRL where FlowRL converges, restores convergence in noisier settings such as red-teaming, and scales cleanly to large MoE backbones. The broader takeaway is that scaling GFlowNet-style RL to large reasoning models may depend less on adding auxiliary machinery and more on identifying which parts of the original objective are unnecessary in this regime.

## Appendix A Limitations and Broader Impacts

GFlowRL’s in-batch Monte Carlo estimate of can have higher variance in principle, especially when the group size is small. In practice, our two stabilizers, importance-sampling correction and flow-gap clipping, help control this variance, and the resulting streamlined estimator consistently outperforms the learned alternative across our evaluated settings. Nevertheless, a more precise characterization of the bias-variance tradeoff, as well as lower-variance group-based estimators, remains an important direction for future work. Another limitation is generality. Our experiments cover math reasoning, code reasoning, and red-teaming for language models, but it remains unclear whether the same estimator-centric design principle extends to broader agentic or multimodal RL settings. We leave this broader validation to future work.

## Appendix C Extended Related Work

RL for LLM post-training and reasoning.
RLHF established sequence-level post-training for aligning language models, typically using PPO-style optimization with a reference-policy regularizer [Ouyang et al., 2022, Schulman et al., 2017]. More recent reasoning systems shift to verifiable rewards on math and code, which makes critic-free or group-based optimization practical at scale. Representative examples include GRPO, REINFORCE++, DAPO, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi k1.5, and DeepCoder [Shao et al., 2024, Hu et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2025b, Guo et al., 2025a, Kimi-Team et al., 2025, Luo et al., 2025]. Many of these systems are built around reward-maximizing or normalized-advantage objectives and have produced strong reasoning performance, but they do not explicitly target a reward-weighted response distribution and can therefore favor a small set of dominant trajectories.

GFlowNets and distribution matching.
GFlowNets were introduced as a framework for sampling compositional objects in proportion to reward rather than greedily maximizing it [Bengio et al., 2021, Bengio et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2022]. Subsequent work developed stronger objectives and interpretations, including trajectory balance, a variational-inference view of GFlowNets, maximum-entropy formulations, entropy-regularized RL connections, and improved training from partial episodes [Malkin et al., 2022, Malkin et al., 2023, Mohammadpour et al., 2024, Tiapkin et al., 2024, Madan et al., 2023], with applications extending to structured decision problems such as robust scheduling [Zhang et al., 2023]. In language modeling, amortized-inference perspectives connect GFlowNet-style objectives to reasoning over latent chains of thought [Hu et al., 2024], and closely related ideas have recently been extended to diffusion-model priors in vision, language, and control [Venkatraman et al., 2024]. The closest prior work for LLM post-training is FOR and FlowRL [Yu et al., 2025a, Zhu et al., 2026]. Our method stays in this distribution-matching line, but departs from FlowRL by removing the learned prompt-conditional partition network and replacing it with a rollout-based estimate motivated by the instability of jointly training that auxiliary model with a frontier-scale policy. Compared to reward-maximizing methods such as GRPO, the GFlowNet objective targets a reference-anchored distribution rather than expected reward, retaining mass on multiple high-reward reasoning paths instead of concentrating on a single mode.

In-batch estimation and variance reduction.
Our estimator is also related to a broader line of work on reducing variance in score-function and policy-gradient optimization. Classical REINFORCE baselines [Williams, 1992, Weaver and Tao, 2001], action-dependent baselines [Wu et al., 2018], and leave-one-out or learned control variates for variational inference and black-box gradient estimation [Richter et al., 2020, Tucker et al., 2017, Grathwohl et al., 2018] all reduce estimator noise without changing the underlying objective. Path-space log-variance objectives further provide low-variance training signals for sampling problems [Nüsken and Richter, 2021], and have been shown to coincide with the trajectory-balance loss for GFlowNets [Malkin et al., 2023], providing additional motivation for variance-aware partition estimates in our setting. In LLM RL, GRPO and REINFORCE++ stabilize critic-free updates through group-relative normalization and global advantage normalization [Shao et al., 2024, Hu et al., 2025], while DAPO emphasizes system-level stability in large-scale reasoning training [Yu et al., 2025b]. Our use of the rollout group is related in spirit to these in-batch estimators, but the quantity we estimate is different: we use the current group to estimate the partition term implied by the distribution-matching objective and inject it as a stop-gradient baseline, structurally analogous to a GRPO group-mean baseline applied to the trajectory-balance residual rather than to the advantage.

Reasoning and safety domains.
Most work on RL post-training for LLMs is evaluated on domains with verifiable rewards, especially mathematical reasoning and code generation. Representative math resources include step-level verification and olympiad-style evaluation [Lightman et al., 2023, He et al., 2024], while code evaluation commonly uses LiveCodeBench, Codeforces, and HumanEval+ [Jain et al., 2025, Penedo et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2021]. We additionally study LLM red-teaming, where rewards are sparser and noisier than in standard reasoning benchmarks. This setting connects our work to jailbreak benchmarks such as AdvBench and HarmBench and to recent automated attack methods including SEMA, FITD, and X-Teaming [Zou et al., 2023, Mazeika et al., 2024, Feng et al., 2026, Weng et al., 2025, Rahman et al., 2025]. We use this domain not primarily to propose a new attack method, but to test whether GFlowRL remains stable when reward feedback is substantially noisier than in math and code.
