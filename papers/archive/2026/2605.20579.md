Will Sawin

###### Abstract.

The basic strategy is the same as the OpenAI team’s proof: To construct a set of points in with many unit distances, it suffices to construct a lattice, and a projection of the lattice to , with many short vectors whose projections to the plane have length , as then the projection to of the intersection of the lattice with a suitable ball will have many unit distances. (Erdős’s original argument used a lattice in , rather than a higher-dimensional lattice with a projection to .)

A useful source of lattices is the rings of integers of number fields, and these carry natural projections to the plane coming from the embeddings of the number field into the complex numbers. When the number field is a CM field , containing a totally real subfield , the squared length of the projection of a lattice vector to the plane lies in . Since has smaller degree than and thus intuitively has fewer elements, it is possible for the projections of many vectors in to have the same length-squared in , and then we can divide by this length to obtain many projections of lattice vectors with length . (For other embeddings, the lengths are given by conjugates of the same elements of , and this lets us control the total length of the vector as well.) One can interpret Erdős’s original argument as showing that this happens when and . Using a similar strategy, combined with a pigeonhole argument to deal with the class group, one can show that this happens for a general if has many small primes that split in and the class number of is not too large. If has many more small primes that split in than has small primes that split in , there is the potential for a better bound than Erdős’s, but one has to control the class number.

One has bounds for the class number in terms of the discriminant, so it therefore suffices to find totally real fields with CM extensions such that has many small primes that split in and the discriminant of is small. To make the construction work, one needs an infinite sequence of fields, and since one wants to take their discriminant small, this requires a sequence of growing degree. The best known construction of a sequence of number fields with increasing degree and discriminant not too large is the Golod-Shafarevich construction of -class field towers of quadratic fields [6], which produces fields of discriminant exponential in the degree.11

The original OpenAI solution used -class field towers of cyclic cubic towers, but the argument was simplified to use -class field towers after a suggestion of Victor Wang. This construction uses the Golod-Shafarevich criterion [6] to show that a certain Galois group is infinite to deduce that there are infinitely many distinct fields in the tower. However, these fields need not have many small primes, since the primes of may have large inertia degree (i.e. residue field extension degree) in these fields. Fortunately, it is possible to modify the construction to bound the inertia degree by applying the Golod-Shafarevich criterion to the Galois group after quotienting by Frobenius elements, obtaining an infinite group in which these Frobenius elements are trivial and thus the inertia degree is . This argument was made, in a different context, by Hajir, Maire, and Ramakrishna [7].

Elements of the strategy have appeared before in work on different problems: The pigeonhole argument is similar to one used by Ellenberg and Venkatesh [2] after a suggestion of Michel and Soundararajan to bound torsion subgroups of class groups, and the idea of constructing high-dimensional lattices with good properties from Golod-Shafarevich towers has been used multiple times [9, 10]. [1] contains a more detailed discussion of the context for this argument.

We improve the above argument in multiple ways: Rather than using the ring of integers as a lattice, we can work with any ideal. This makes the pigeonhole argument easier. Rather than using the class number, we can consider the relative class number, which is smaller. Rather than quotienting by Frobenius elements, we can quotient by a fixed power of the Frobenius element, forcing the inertia degree to be small instead of at most , but making it much easier to apply the Golod-Shafarevich criterion (this argument was also made by Hajir, Maire, and Ramakrishna [7]). We discuss other improvements after giving the proof.

This paper itself is likely far from optimized. It could be possible to improve the lower bound using techniques from analytic number theory, proving a more precise version of the key class group bound [11, Corollary 3] for the relevant number fields, using techniques from algebraic number theory, showing that there exist infinite towers of number fields satisfying a different set of local conditions, or using computational techniques, searching for better values of the parameters .

Finally, we discuss two further questions, both suggested by Thomas Bloom: what is the density of the set of for which a set of points in Theorem 1 exists, and what are the limits to the exponents that can be achieved by this argument?

## 2. Discussion

We first explain in more detail how our arguments differ from those [13] obtained by Lijie Chen using an internal OpenAI model, with a simplification suggested by Victor Wang:

In Lemma 2, we apply the probabilistic method to bound the ratio instead of the value . Note that the improvement here is proportional to the ratio between the average value of and the upper bound on obtained in the argument, and the upper bound on obtained in either argument is much larger than the average value.

In Lemma 4, we work with an ideal instead of simply the ring of integers.

In Lemma 7, the pigeonhole argument, we make several improvements. First, we do not require the prime ideals of to be split over . This assumption was made in the OpenAI team’s proof and likely led to significant loss as it means one can only use primes split in the field extension , and the smallest primes split in this extension are typically exponentially large in . Second, we introduce the parameter that allows taking powers of the prime ideal, allowing one to get more leverage out of fewer primes. (This idea was also used to simplify the argument in the human-created writeup [1].) Third, the fact that we work with a fractional ideal means we don’t have to embed the optimal fractional ideal into a principal ideal, incurring further loss. Fourth, once we obtain a large number of elements whose norms to generate the same ideal, we do not divide them by their complex conjugates to obtain elements with the same norm, but instead use the unit group to control them, in the form of Lemma 6. Fifth, we use the relative class group instead of the class group for pigeonholing.

In Lemma 9, we use [11] instead of the Minkowski bound to get an improved bound on the (relative) class number.

In Lemma 11, we work with the maximal unramified extension of instead of the maximal extension of ramified only at the primes in , which has substantially smaller root discriminant. We also avoid the assumption that are congruent to mod , which was used only to ensure that is unramified at , so is unramified, by instead ensuring that is ramified at .

The flexibility to use primes that are not split is used heavily in the proof of Theorem 1. In fact, every prime in is either inert or ramified in .
