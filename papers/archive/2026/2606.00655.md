###### Abstract

The burgeoning field of LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) promises to tackle complex tasks through collaborative intelligence, yet fundamental questions regarding their scaling behavior and intrinsic collective dynamics remain underexplored. This paper systematically investigates how the performance of a homogeneous MAS evolves as the number of agents increases, isolating the variable of collaboration from model or knowledge heterogeneity. We propose the Sequential Iterative Multi-Agent System (SIMAS) framework, a minimalist architecture centered on sequential inter-agent communication, to clearly observe scaling effects. Through extensive experiments across diverse tasks and model scales, we establish that MAS performance does not scale monotonically with agent count but follows a pattern of diminishing returns, governed by a trade-off between collaborative synergy and coordination overhead. Our findings reveal that effective MAS requires a sufficiently capable base LLM, that task type critically modulates the optimal agent count, and that collective intelligence is an emergent property contingent on strategic interaction design rather than a guaranteed outcome of agent plurality. The performance degradation stems coordination overhead rather than merely long-context failure, and the scaling tendency generalizes across interaction architectures like structured debate topologies. This work provides a foundational understanding of MAS scaling laws, offering practical guidance for designing efficient collaborative systems and challenging the prevailing assumption that more agents invariably lead to better performance.

## 1 Introduction

In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in text generation, complex reasoning, and decision-making, establishing themselves as the core foundation for constructing intelligent systems, often referred to as ”agents”.
Though individual agent has exhibited excellent problem-solving ability in expansive fields with performance enhanced—for instance, through Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting Wei et al. (2022) to elicit step-by-step reasoning, or by enabling models to leverage external APIs via frameworks like Toolformer Schick et al. (2023), however, many real-world challenges, such as sophisticated software development or multi-faceted problem-solving, inherently require collaborative efforts.
This necessity has driven the emergence of LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), a field that has rapidly evolved from early exploratory frameworks to complex systems Xi et al. (2023); Luo et al. (2025), where multiple agents interact to achieve common goals.

In MAS, collaboration enables agents to share knowledge, assign specialized roles, and integrate their complementary strengths, thereby tackling tasks beyond the scope of any single agent—ranging from handling extensive contexts Zhang et al. (2025) to complex social simulations Qian et al. (2023).
This vision is supported by a growing series of frameworks:
AutoGen Wu et al. (2023) facilitates customizable agent conversations, CAMEL Li et al. (2023) explores role-playing for idea exploration, and MetaGPT Hong et al. (2023) assigns standardized workflows to simulate a software company.
These systems often deploy agents as ”reasoning experts” or ”fact-checkers” to boost collective output.
However, despite these engineering advances and recent efforts to probe the robustness of agent communication He et al. (2025), a fundamental scientific understanding of MAS collective behavior remains under-explored.
First, in order to isolate the scaling effects of collaboration without the confounding variable of model heterogeneity, we ask: when all agents are built using the same underlying language model, does increasing the number of agents consistently improve the system’s performance?
Second, are there common patterns in how these systems operate, regardless of how they are designed?
Third, how do the collaboration of MAS differ from the reasoning of a single agent?

To provide a systematic analysis of the scaling laws in collaboration of MAS, we establish a investigation into how the performance of MAS evolves as the number of agents increases.
We propose the Sequential Iterative Multi-Agent System (SIMAS) framework, which strips away scenario-specific components and retains only sequential inter-agent communication as its core mechanism.
This design minimizes architectural complexity, enabling clear observation of performance scaling with agent count and identification of general patterns in MAS.
Through a rigorous comparison between MAS and single-agent baselines, we aim to quantitatively delineate the boundaries where collaboration yields positive returns versus where it introduces diminishing returns.

Our investigation yields several principal findings:
Model capability is a prerequisite for effective MAS.
We observe that only models above a certain performance threshold (typically larger-scale models) can effectively power MAS.
LLM-based MAS scalability is governed by a fundamental trade-off between collaborative synergy and coordination overhead.
This reframes the goal from ”more agents” to ”optimal number,” highlighting that excessive collaboration can be detrimental, particularly in reasoning-intensive tasks where focus and coherence are critical; performance degradations derive from coordination overhead rather than merely long-context failure, and the tendency generalizes across interaction architectures like structured debate topologies.
Collective intelligence in MAS is an emergent property contingent on interaction architecture, not an automatic outcome of agent plurality.
Without strategic architectural design, an MAS risks achieving only the illusion of collaboration while failing to surpass the capabilities of a well-prompted individual.

## 2 Related Works

### 2.1 Architectures for Multi-Agent Collaboration

The foundation of MAS lies in defining how agents interact to extend the capabilities of individual LLM.
Early research focused on unstructured communicative frameworks.
For instance, CAMEL Li et al. (2023) introduced ”inception prompting” to facilitate autonomous role-playing, demonstrating that distinct personas can guide solution exploration.
Building on this, AutoGen Wu et al. (2023) provided a flexible infrastructure for dynamic conversations between agents, while MetaGPT Hong et al. (2023) incorporated Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) with rigid role assignments to streamline complex workflows like software generation.
Similarly, AgentVerse Chen et al. (2023) and the recent Agent-Pro Zhang et al. (2024) introduce iterative optimization mechanisms, where agents engage in ”reflect-and-refine” loops to correct errors dynamically.

Despite the diversity of existing multi-agent architectures, they all fundamentally rely on a core collaborative process involving iterative communication, shared contextual memory, and sequential reasoning Xi et al. (2025); Xi et al. (2023); Guo et al. (2024). Advanced mechanisms such as dynamic reflection are merely engineering scaffolds to mitigate the inherent friction of this core, meaning all such systems belong to the same interactive paradigm Talebirad and Nadiri (2023). By designing SIMAS as a minimalist system that isolates this core Xi et al. (2025), we reveal that the trade-off between collaborative synergy and coordination overhead is a universal property of all multi-agent systems, challenging the assumption that adding more agents always improves performance and underscoring the importance of architectural design.

### 2.2 Scaling Behavior in Multi-Agent Systems

Recently, the community has begun to formally investigate the scaling laws and inherent limitations of LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS).
A notable concurrent work Qian et al. (2025) explores scaling multi-agent collaboration by structuring agents into a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) topology, termed MacNet.
By enforcing localized information passing they successfully mitigate context explosion and observe a logistic growth pattern as the system scales to hundreds or thousands of agents.

Conversely, other recent studies have begun to critically examine the inherent vulnerabilities of MAS as they scale.
 Flint et al. (2025) investigate group size effects, revealing that expanding agent counts can paradoxically exacerbate collective misalignment and consensus failure.
Similarly,  Cemri et al. (2025) provide a comprehensive taxonomy of why MAS fail, highlighting critical issues such as reasoning fragmentation, compounding hallucinations, and cascading errors during complex interactions.
Complementary to the coordination overhead perspective, a concurrent work by Shehata and Li (2026) reveals an orthogonal failure mode: the algorithmic “Bystander Effect” in MAS, where simulated social pressure triggers cognitive loafing: agents abandon independent reasoning to sycophantically comply with swarm consensus even when internally deriving correct answers.
Together, these works establish that multi-agent collaboration faces both coordination and social-psychological challenges, with effective MAS requiring architectural safeguards against both.

While these studies offer crucial insights, they leave a critical gap in understanding the raw, unmitigated scaling dynamics of MAS.
Our work bridges this gap by introducing the SIMAS framework as a deliberate, minimalist control group. By intentionally stripping away complex topologies, information-filtering mechanisms, and structured aggregation, SIMAS utilizes pure sequential communication with progressive history accumulation to empirically quantify the tension collaborative synergy and coordination overhead to demonstrate the scaling behavior of unstructured multi-agent dialogue.

### 3.3 Setting of Single Agent

In this experiment, each agent was driven by LLM and assigned descriptions, personalities, beliefs, and strengths, with the descriptions set as ”the i-th assistent in the group chat” and therefore not considered as influencing factors.
All agents are powered by the same LLM, sharing identical model parameters and knowledge base.
To introduce diversity in reasoning styles, each agent is assigned a unique profile before the discussion begins.

The profile consists of three attributes: personality, core beliefs, and expertise. Personality is a general disposition (e.g., ”Skeptical” or ”Aggressive”) that guides the tone and focus of their reasoning, expertise is a specific domain of knowledge (e.g., ”Data Interpretation” or ”Logical Reasoning”) they are instructed to prioritize, and core beliefs are a set of principles (e.g., ”Precision outweighs speed”) that influence their approach to problem-solving.

These attributes are automatically generated by the language model for each agent to simulate a group of diverse experts. The specific configurations and formal definitions of agent attributes are detailed in Appendix A.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

To systematically examine the scaling effects of agent collaboration, we vary the number of agents from 1 to 8 in increments of 1.
This granular range allows us to capture the nuanced relationship between team size and performance, and it directly aligns with the practical band of deployed MAS like AutoGen, where the synergy–overhead trade-off is most critical.
Regarding agent configurations, we established a baseline condition where agents possess personality traits, expertise, and beliefs.

For performance evaluation, we employ three complementary benchmarks, each targeting distinct cognitive capabilities:
The Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) dataset Hendrycks et al. (2021), covering 57 diverse subjects across STEM, humanities, and social sciences, to assess general knowledge and broad reasoning capabilities.
The E-KAR dataset Luo et al. (2022), specifically designed for evaluating analogical reasoning through entity-based knowledge graph analogies.
The AIME 2025 Mathematical Association of America (2025) competition problems, consisting of advanced mathematical reasoning challenges, which we use in Section 5 to compare MAS against single-agent reasoning on highly complex tasks.

Note that in Section 5 for external literature comparison, we will exclusively reference additional datasets such as GSM8K, MATH, and AIME 2024 and the data is directly cited from Gu et al. (2025) rather than our own primary experiments.

From MMLU, we select 6 representative disciplines (see Table 3) and sample 17 questions each to balance efficiency with robustness.
Each experimental configuration is repeated three times, with results averaged to ensure stability.

We employ two widely-used, open-source model families: Llama-3.1 AI (2024) and Qwen2.5 Yang et al. (2024).
To understand the impact of model scale, we use versions with 8B/7B and 70B/72B parameters from each family, and due to computational cost and inference speed constraints, we did not evaluate larger models beyond 70B parameters.
These models provide a strong baseline for general-purpose reasoning, allowing for a clear analysis of multi-agent scaling effects with balance of capabilities and generation speed.

To isolate the contribution of each attribute, we compare this baseline against four alternative configurations: one lacking all three attributes, and three configurations each removing exactly one of the three attributes.
This structured ablation enables us to analyze how each factor contributes to system performance and stability.
For these ablation studies, we specifically use the Llama-3.1-70B model with agent number , chosen to represent key points in the scaling range while reducing experimental overhead.

To address the potential confound between agent count and context length, we conduct a controlled token-padding experiment (Section 4.3) where total input length is fixed across all agent counts using neutral filler tokens.
To assess cross-architectural generality, we implement a parallel AutoGen GroupChat debate baseline (Section 4.4) with the same model–dataset combinations.

## 6 Conclusion

This work systematically demonstrates that LLM-based Multi-Agent System performance does not scale linearly with agent count but exhibits a pattern of diminishing returns. Effective collaboration first requires a sufficiently capable base LLM. The optimal number of agents is a critical design parameter, heavily dependent on task type and model architecture, balancing synergy against overhead. The performance degradations derived from collaboration overhead generalizes across interaction architectures. Crucially, collective intelligence is not an automatic outcome of adding agents but an emergent property contingent on deliberate interaction design. Without architectural support for synthesis and refinement, multi-agent dialogue risks inefficiency. Future MAS development must therefore prioritize designing adaptive, task-aware collaboration protocols over simply increasing agent plurality.

## Limitations

This study, while providing foundational insights into MAS scaling behavior, has several limitations that point to future research directions.
First, our focus on homogeneous agents using a single LLM isolates collaboration effects but does not capture the heterogeneous model and tool use common in practical systems, where complementarity might alter scaling dynamics.
Second, our minimalist SIMAS architecture, while clarifying first principles, omits sophisticated coordination mechanisms (e.g., voting, dynamic workflows) that could mitigate the overhead we observed.
While our AutoGen comparison (Section 4.4) confirms the inverted-U law’s cross-architectural generality, exploring a broader range of topologies (DAG-based, hierarchical) and their interaction with model capability remains an open direction.
Third, although our token-padding experiment (Section 4.3) confirms that coordination overhead rather than long-context failure drives the inverted-U pattern, we evaluated only output accuracy; deeper psycholinguistic analysis of agent dialogues—quantifying semantic diversity, conversational dynamics, and belief divergence—could further characterize the micro-level mechanisms of collaboration breakdown.
Fourth, our evaluation primarily uses closed-book QA benchmarks, which may not fully capture collaborative benefits in longitudinal, creative, or tool-augmented tasks.
Finally, scaling was tested only up to 8 agents; the dynamics of much larger collectives and their potential for novel emergent phenomena remain unexplored.

## Ethical Concerns

This study focuses on the algorithmic and architectural principles of multi-agent scaling, utilizing exclusively publicly available and widely-adopted benchmark datasets (e.g., MMLU, AIME, E-KAR).
No sensitive personal data, simulated social dynamics, or real-world decision-making scenarios are involved. The Sequential Iterative Multi-Agent System (SIMAS) framework is designed as a minimalist research tool to investigate fundamental collaboration dynamics, explicitly excluding applications in opinion manipulation, autonomous action with real-world consequences, or the generation of deceptive content.
All experimental interactions are confined to closed, controlled environments for problem-solving, and no user privacy information is stored in our codebase or logs; only aggregated performance metrics are reported.
During the research process, AI assistants (including DeepSeek-R1) were employed as tools to aid in specific auxiliary tasks such as brainstorming initial ideas, generating code for experimental pipelines, polishing textual descriptions, and creating visualizations. We explicitly state that all core research ideas, experimental design, data analysis, interpretation of results, and scientific conclusions were originated, critically evaluated, and decisively finalized by the human authors. The AI tools served strictly as supportive instruments, and every piece of their output was rigorously reviewed, validated, and often substantially revised by the authors to ensure correctness and alignment with the research objectives.
The primary ethical considerations—such as potential reasoning bias, coherence fragmentation, or the amplification of base model errors—are emergent properties stemming from the underlying LLM capabilities and the chosen task prompts, rather than inherent flaws of the SIMAS architecture itself.
Should the principles explored here inform the design of future MAS deployed in open or user-facing contexts, rigorous additional risk assessment and governance mechanisms would be mandatory, adhering strictly to established ethical guidelines in AI research and development.

## Appendix B Framework of SIMAS

### B.1 Problem Provider

The Problem Provider module loads datasets (e.g., MMLU, E-KAR), preprocesses questions to ensure consistency, and normalizes answers by mapping responses to standardized formats (e.g., options A/B/C/D).

### B.2 Agent Generator

The Agent Generator takes as inputs the discipline type of the problem and the number of agents , and outputs dynamically generated agents. These agents are created by invoking the LLM API with tailored prompts to assign unique roles and personalities.

### B.3 Group Chat Engine

The Group Chat Engine executes the main workflow of SIMAS, managing multi-round discussions. It monitors for timeouts or errors and implements a retry mechanism with a maximum of 100 retries (max_retries=100).

### B.4 Evaluator

The Evaluator compares the final group chat answer with the ground truth and records auxiliary metrics, such as confidence scores, to assess response quality.

The framework leverages the LLM’s API for text generation and processing, offering the following key functionalities: It supports Agent Generation by automatically creating agents with distinct personalities and expertise based on specified quantities and role requirements. Its Group Chat Management orchestrates communication among agents, ensuring sequential contributions and maintaining discussion history for context-aware responses. The Answer Evaluation functionality extracts the final answer from the last agent’s response after group chat completion and compares it with the ground truth to compute accuracy.

The framework supports seamless switching between different language models, enabling comparative experiments across models. This flexibility ensures robust evaluation of multi-agent system performance under varying configurations.

## Appendix C Mechanisms of U-Shaped Recovery

A notable deviation from the canonical inverted-U pattern occurs in Philosophy with Llama3.1-70B, where accuracy exhibits a U-shaped curve: declining from the single-agent baseline through intermediate agent counts before partially recovering at . Qualitative analysis of agent dialogues on an MMLU item concerning Aristotle’s concept of incontinence (knowing the wrong and doing it anyway) reveals a three-phase mechanism.

In Phase 1 ( –3), dialogue remains focused and efficient: one agent dominates with correct reasoning while coordination overhead is minimal. In Phase 2 ( –5), social meta-discourse (apologies, acknowledgments, redundant praise) proliferates and divergent minority interpretations emerge—for instance, conflating akrasia with the distinct concept of enkrateia. This fragments the reasoning chain and causes the accuracy dip. In Phase 3 ( ), the sheer volume of majority agents reiterating the correct pre-training-consistent answer statistically suppresses earlier noise, producing an apparent recovery. This recovery, however, constitutes pseudo-stability rather than genuine collaborative intelligence, as it depends on the model’s pre-training bias toward the consensual answer rather than on structured synthesis of diverse perspectives. Qwen2.5-72B does not exhibit the U-shape, attributable to its lower semantic diversity and stronger long-context robustness.

This case reveals a key distinction: error-propagating tasks (mathematics, formal logic), where a single incorrect step irreversibly derails the solution, exhibit classic inverted-U patterns with steep post-peak declines; consensus-recoverable tasks (interpretive philosophy, fact retrieval with high prior agreement), where majority voting over redundant correct responses can mask mid-scale coordination failures, may exhibit U-shaped recovery contingent on model-specific semantic diversity.
