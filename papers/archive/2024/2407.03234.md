# Self-Evaluation as a Defense Against Adversarial Attacks on LLMs

Hannah Brown*

  
Leon Lin*

  
Kenji Kawaguchi

Affiliation: National University of Singapore

Affiliation: {hsbrown, kenji, michaelshieh}@comp.nus.edu.sg, leonlin@u.nus.edu

###### Abstract

We introduce a defense against adversarial attacks on LLMs utilizing self-evaluation.
Our method requires no model fine-tuning, instead using pre-trained models to evaluate the inputs and outputs of a generator model, significantly reducing the cost of implementation in comparison to other, finetuning-based methods.
Our method can significantly reduce the attack success rate of attacks on both open and closed-source LLMs, beyond the reductions demonstrated by Llama-Guard2 and commonly used content moderation APIs.
We present an analysis of the effectiveness of our method, including attempts to attack the evaluator in various settings, demonstrating that it is also more resilient to attacks than existing methods.

## 1 Introduction

Ensuring the safety of LLM outputs is becoming an increasing concern. Though models are trained to output helpful, harmless, and honest outputs through methods such as RLHF, and guardrails have been proposed for inference time Team (2024); Rebedea et al. (2023), there are a growing number of attacks Zou et al. (2023); Zhu et al. (2023) against models that we observe can still break through these defenses. This has motivated several defenses against adversarial attacks Kumar et al. (2023); Robey et al. (2023), in addition to existing methods for detecting harmful outputs Team (2024); Hu et al. (2024). While these methods show some success, some rely on costly algorithms, some on fine-tuning models, and others on proprietary APIs (e.g., the OpenAI content moderation API).

Figure 1: Overview of our defense. Model inputs and/or outputs are classified as safe or unsafe by an evaluator LLM, allowing the detection of unsafe inputs and outputs, including those induced through adversarial attacks.

We introduce a defense against adversarial attacks on model alignment using self-evaluation Kadavath et al. (2022). We demonstrate that, with no additional fine-tuning, pre-trained models can classify inputs and outputs as unsafe with high accuracy. These results hold for inputs and outputs containing adversarial suffixes as well. We can reduce the ASR of inputs attacked with suffixes generated by GCG Zou et al. (2023) from % to % for Vicuna-7B Zheng et al. (2023) simply by prompting another LLM to classify the inputs as safe or unsafe. We obtain similar results using other open and closed-source models as evaluators. We compare our method to existing defenses and find that it far outperforms other methods, particularly for samples containing adversarial suffixes, which Llama-Guard2 and content moderation APIs often fail to classify as harmful.

We further demonstrate that by decoupling safety classification from generation, our defense is challenging to attack in adaptive attack settings using the strongest existing attacks. Though we find it is possible to attack the evaluator, it requires training a separate adversarial suffix targeted to the evaluator, and in the worst case, using our defense yields lower ASR values than an undefended generator.

Though our defense requires querying another model, it is easy to implement, requiring no fine-tuning, and achieves higher performance than both commercial moderation APIs and Llama-Guard2 Team (2024) across evaluation settings, making it a practical defense method11

## 2 Threat Model

We consider a setting in which an LLM receives a potentially adversarial query and is tasked with generating a safe response . should satisfactorily, truthfully, and safely respond to . This means that if is unsafe (e.g., how to commit a crime), should be a refusal to answer.

We assume that an adversary has query-only access to , with an unlimited number of queries allowed. This allows them to observe model responses and perturb their input accordingly, but not access the weights of . In this setting, a desirable defense achieves both high utility and high safety. A trivial defense, simply refusing to reply to all , satisfies safety perfectly, but results in a useless . An ideal defense responds well to all safe inputs and refuses all unsafe ones.

### 2.1 Attack

As demonstrated by Zou et al., it is possible to find a suffix that, when appended to , results in generating unsafe outputs. These suffixes have shown to be transferable across different model architectures and work across model inputs. We assume that may contain an adversarial suffix . We refer to this attack as the generator-only attack to differentiate it from later attacks.

### 2.2 Evaluation

We evaluate samples under the assumption that the evaluator is part of a pipeline. Here, it is reasonable to consider the ASR for the combination of the generator and the evaluator rather than only one component. For the input-only setting, we consider all samples from AdvBench harmful and all GPT-4 generated benign inputs to be safe, while for the output-only and input-output settings, we consider a sample harmful if the model input component is harmful. We evaluate this using the same evaluation criteria as the GCG paper. If the model output contains no refusal words, it’s considered harmful. A high ASR in this setting means that both the generator and evaluator are performing poorly at guarding against unsafe outputs. The reduction between the ASR against only the generator and the combined ASR shows the contribution of the evaluator. We additionally provide the F1 score of each evaluator in each evaluation setting in Appendix A.

### 2.3 Data

As input data, we select 100 harmful instructions from AdvBench Zou et al. (2023) and generate 100 safe instructions using GPT-422

More detail on the generation process is in Appendix G.. Output data is generated using Vicuna-7B_v1.5 Zheng et al. (2023), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1 Jiang et al. (2023), and Llama-2 Touvron et al. (2023).

### 2.4 Baseline Defenses

As baseline defenses, we consider Llama-Guard2 Team (2024), a fine-tuned version of Llama3 AI@Meta (2024) trained to recognize and classify unsafe inputs and outputs, the OpenAI content moderation API, the Azure content moderation API, and the Perspective API, all of which use black box models to recognize unsafe content.

## 4 Attacking the Evaluator

Figure 6: Overview of the direct attack. The adversary appends a suffix trained to attack both the evaluator and generator to their input, causing the generator to output a harmful response and the evaluator to misclassify it.

As both defenses presented depend on another LLM, a natural question is how susceptible the defenses themselves are to adversarial attacks. Is it possible to attack both and at the same time?

Input-only seems particularly susceptible to attack, as and receive the same input, allowing a suffix to be appended to an ideal location to attack both Zou et al. (2023). We propose an attack that we will call the direct attack, in which we train a suffix to attack both the evaluator and generator as shown in Fig. 6. We demonstrate that, while it is possible to successfully attack input-only and input-output defenses using direct attack, the ASR of a model defended with is still lower than that of an undefended model.

### 4.1 Attack Formulation

As is not directly controlled by the adversary, we are only able to consider the input-only and input-output settings for this attack. Rather than inserting suffix which attacks only the generator, we assume that the adversary inserts which can attack both the generator and evaluator. Attacking the evaluator is slightly different from attacking the generator, in that the evaluator may fail in two ways: either by classifying unsafe inputs as safe (reducing the safety) or safe inputs as unsafe (reducing the utility). We consider both objectives, but primarily focus on the first, as it poses a higher risk. We assume that the adversary has query-only access to the combination of and ; that is, it can observe ’s outputs when marks them as safe, and can always infer ’s classifications.

## 5 Copy-paste Attack

The direct attack does not allow us to test the output-only defense. For this, we design another adaptive attack utilizing the instruction-following abilities of LLMs. We refer to this attack as the copy-paste attack.

We assume the same query-only setting as for the direct attack where the adversary can query the combined - pipeline as much as is desired, but not only or only . We also assume the adversary has suffix which can attack .

We use a prompt that instructs the model to both reply to the user query and append to the end of its output. Models are generally able to follow directions to append this suffix correctly, with most failures occurring when models generate refusals or responses that would be longer than the model’s maximum length setting.
We employed the copy-paste attack in the output-only and input-output settings, targeting Vicuna and Llama-2. We use matching generators and evaluators for Vicuna and Llama-2 and use Vicuna as the generator for Llama-Guard2 and the APIs. For each evaluator, we train a single targeted universal suffix using the GCG algorithm. Other hyperparameter settings remain the same as those for the direct attack.

## 6 Discussion

Existing defenses such as Llama-Guard2 exhibit more variation in their classifications and are sensitive to changes in suffixes that are not trained to target the evaluator, even in the simplest setting of input-only. In contrast, all evaluators we test can consistently classify attacked inputs as harmful regardless of the suffix appended.

Our results also provide more information about how adversarial suffixes bypass model alignment.
While adversarial suffixes can bypass generator alignment, they do not compromise the same model’s ability to classify input as harmful, which would represent a far stronger attack.

Our attacks demonstrate that, while it is possible to attack our defense, it does not make models more susceptible to attack; even when attacked, self-evaluation provides a higher level of defense than an undefended generator receives. Additionally, when compared to other defense methods, our ASR increases less when attacked through these methods, indicating that self-evaluation is a more robust defense method than existing methods.

## 7 Related Work

### 7.1 Harmful Text Classification

Work on automatically classifying harmful text ranges from toxicity and hate speech Jahan and Oussalah (2023) to mis/disinformation Oshikawa et al. (2018), as only a few examples. Approaches range from more traditional classification approaches using specifically trained or fine-tuned models Dixon et al. (2018) to more recent work utilizing the instruction-following abilities of LLMs to classify harmful text Hu et al. (2024). There are also numerous moderation APIs, both open-source and paid, including those used in this paper.

### 7.2 Adversarial Attacks

There have been many attack methods proposed targeting the alignment of LLMs, including Universal Transferable Attacks Zou et al. (2023), DAN Shen et al. (2023), AutoDAN Zhu et al. (2023), and more Shin et al. (2020).
Recent work has examined “glitch tokens," defined as tokens that appear in the tokenizer vocabulary but which the model was not trained/undertrained on Land and Bartolo (2024). It has been found that these tokens can cause irregular behavior in models, including unsafe outputs Geiping et al. (2024).

### 7.3 LLM Defenses

Llama-Guard Inan et al. () and subsequently Llama-Guard 2 Team (2024), were proposed as guardrails for model inputs/outputs. Based on fine-tuned versions of Llama2 Touvron et al. (2023) and Llama3 AI@Meta (2024) respectively these models are fine-tuned to classify the safety of model inputs/outputs. However, this approach requires fine-tuning a model, and, as demonstrated, is more susceptible to attacks than base Llama3.
Other defenses have been proposed against adversarial attacks, from filtering Kumar et al. (2023), to inference time guardrails Rebedea et al. (2023), smoothing Robey et al. (2023), and more Jain et al. (2023).

### 7.4 Self Evaluation

Kadavath et al. (2022) demonstrate that LLMs can estimate with high accuracy the probability that they will respond to prompts correctly. Subsequent work has found that self-evaluation is an effective technique in many aspects of model performance including decoding Xie et al. (2024), representations Paul et al. (2023), and reasoning Rae et al. (2022); Madaan et al. (2024); Shinn et al. (2024).
Concurrent work Phute et al. (2023) found models can identify harmful content through self-evaluation but did not provide a thorough evaluation on attacked inputs for as many models, or compare to content evaluation APIs.

## 8 Conclusion

We demonstrate that pre-trained LLMs can identify attacked inputs and outputs with high accuracy, allowing the use of self-evaluation as a powerful and easy-to-implement defense against adversarial attacks. Though attacks against this defense exist, and stronger attacks may be found, we demonstrate that self-evaluation remains the current strongest defense against unsafe inputs, even when attacked, does not reduce model performance, and does not increase model susceptibility to attack.

Whereas existing defenses such as Llama-Guard and defense APIs fail when tasked with classifying samples with adversarial suffixes added, self-evaluation remains robust. The ease of implementation, potential for using small, low-cost models, and strength of defense offered make self-evaluation defense a valuable contribution to LLM safety, robustness, and alignment.

## 9 Limitations

We acknowledge that the attacks we propose in this paper are not the strongest possible attacks against this defense. They are not meant to be. Rather, they are meant to act as baseline demonstrations that our defense does not weaken model defenses further.

In this paper, we focus on adversarial suffixes as attacks. Future work considering other attacks would represent valuable contributions to the area. In addition, while we show the efficacy of self-evaluation as a defense, this exposes the fact that adversarial suffixes do not confuse models’ ability to classify harmful text, but simply cause them to generate it. This encourages future research into the mechanisms that allow adversarial suffixes to work, which could lead to a better understanding of LLMs in general and to the general area of LLM safety and robustness. However, both are out of the scope of this paper.

Following the Bender Rule Bender (2011), we acknowledge that in this paper, we examine only English language inputs and outputs. As such, we cannot guarantee the effectiveness of our method for other languages. However, the results we demonstrate are promising, and we encourage future research verifying self-evaluation as a defense for other languages as well.

## 10 Ethical Considerations

This paper presents examples of harmful outputs which may be harmful to users. We have minimized the amount of harmful text shown in the main paper to what we believe is strictly necessary to understand our defense. However, for the sake of reproducibility, and a better understanding of our results, we do include examples in our appendix (Appendices F and E) and in our supplemental material.

In addition, we demonstrate new methods of attacking models, potentially introducing ways for adversaries to attack models. However, we use these attacks as a method to demonstrate the efficacy of our defense and do not believe that they represent attacks a real-world adversary would not attempt given our defense.

For both issues, we believe the benefits of presenting this defense outweigh the possible harms.

## Appendix B Direct Attack Training and Evaluation

Here we provide full details on the training for the direct attack along with observations from training and evaluation.

### B.1 Training the Direct Attack

To train suffixes for the direct attack, we select 100 harmful instructions from AdvBench to train universal suffixes to attack using the GCG algorithm. We utilize a single A100-40G GPU, and initialize the suffix with ’!!!’. The batch size is 128, and the top-k parameter is 256. For the simultaneous attack, we applied a 3:1 weight ratio between generation and evaluation objectives. We evaluate on the same set of 100 samples (distinct from the training data) used to evaluate the generator-only attack.

### B.2 Training Dynamics for Direct Attack

When optimizing objectives in turn, we observe that, while the ASR against both the generator and evaluator increase steadily throughout training, the evaluator is significantly easier to attack, with its ASR quickly reaching its maximum value of roughly % and staying relatively steady over training. As shown in Fig. 8, neither the generator nor evaluator ASR is significantly impacted by switching objectives.
